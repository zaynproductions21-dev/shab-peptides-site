Housing disrepair · Checking a firm

Before you give a housing disrepair firm your details, check which register they're on

Someone rang about the damp in the back bedroom. They knew the name of the housing association. They said no win, no fee, and that there could be thousands in it. Now they want the full address, a phone number, photographs, and the name of whoever has been ignoring the repair reports. Before any of that goes anywhere, there is a two-minute check — and most people run it on the wrong register.

A business that handles housing disrepair claims in England and Wales must be either authorised by the Financial Conduct Authority as a claims management company, or regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority as a law firm. One or the other. A business that is on neither register should not be taking a tenant's details at all.

General information about how the registers work, not advice on any particular claim.

The check most people run tells them the wrong thing

The instinct is reasonable: search the FCA register, and if the name isn't there, assume something is wrong. That test produces the wrong answer in both directions.

A solicitors' firm doing housing disrepair work is not on the FCA register, and that is entirely correct. Law firms are excluded from FCA claims management authorisation precisely because they are already regulated — by the SRA, under rules that are in some respects stricter. Searching the FCA register for a legitimate law firm returns nothing, and reading that as a warning sign means walking away from the better-regulated of the two options.

It fails the other way too. A caller saying "our panel of solicitors" is not telling you they are a solicitor. It usually means the opposite: that the business is a lead generator, that it will pass the details to a law firm it has a commercial arrangement with, and that the people on the phone are not the people who would run the case. That arrangement can be perfectly legitimate — but only if the business doing the seeking out and referring is itself FCA-authorised for it.

So the real question is not "are they on the FCA register". It is which register they should be on, and are they on it.

Why both registers exist, and which one applies

Claims management regulation moved to the FCA on 1 April 2019. The FCA regulates claims management activity across six prescribed sectors, and housing disrepair is one of them. The others are personal injury, financial services and products, specified benefits, criminal injuries, and employment.

What counts as regulated activity is broader than most people assume. It is not limited to running the case. It expressly includes seeking out, referrals and identification of claims or potential claims, alongside advice, investigation and representation. In plain terms: finding tenants with damp and passing them to a solicitor is itself the regulated activity. A business doing only that still needs authorisation.

The exception is the one that explains the confusion. Article 89N of the Regulated Activities Order excludes claims management activity carried on in the ordinary course of legal practice by certain legal professionals, including solicitors. Where that exclusion applies, the activity is not a regulated claims management activity and FCA authorisation is not required — the firm is regulated by the SRA instead. The exclusion only holds while the practitioner is acting under the professional rules they are subject to.

Hence two registers, and a different one depending on what the business actually is.

How to actually run the search

  1. Get the legal name, not the brand. Websites trade under names that are not the registered company. Ask for the registered company name and company number, or take them from the website's terms or privacy policy. A business that will not give you either has answered the question.
  2. Search that name on the right register. The FCA register and the SRA register both search on name; the FCA entry also shows the Companies House number, which is what confirms you have the right entity rather than a similarly named one.
  3. Read the status, not just the presence of a result. This is the step that gets skipped. The FCA register keeps firms listed after their permission ends, marked "No longer authorised" with the date it stopped. An entry existing is not the same as an entry being current.
  4. Check the website says so too. An authorised claims management company has to identify itself as FCA-authorised and give its FRN. A solicitors' firm gives its SRA number. A site in a regulated sector carrying neither, anywhere, is a finding in itself.

A detail worth knowing. The FCA register entry carries the firm's own complaints contact and address. If those do not match what the caller or the website told you — a different town, a different company number, a name the caller has never mentioned — you are probably looking at a different business trading on a brand that was authorised once.

What you decide, once you know

It is an FCA-authorised claims company

Legitimate, and you can proceed. Understand what you are buying: a CMC is not your solicitor and does not give legal advice. It will take a fee, usually a percentage of compensation, which is capped for most claims management work. Ask what the fee is and when it is charged before anything is signed.

The losing move: treating the CMC's assessment of your case as legal advice, and not asking what the fee is until the compensation arrives.

It is an SRA-regulated law firm

You are dealing directly with the people who would run the case, under professional rules, with the Legal Ombudsman available if something goes wrong. Ask who at the firm will have conduct of the matter, and get the funding arrangement in writing before work starts.

The losing move: assuming "no win, no fee" means no deductions at all. Ask what comes out of the compensation and what happens if the claim fails.

It is a lead generator that intends to pass you on

Ask two things: are they FCA-authorised to do that, and which firm will the details go to. Then check that firm as well. A referral into a good firm is fine. A referral you cannot trace is a set of personal details — your address, your health, your housing situation — in the hands of a business you cannot identify.

The losing move: giving the details first and asking who gets them afterwards.

It is on neither register

Stop there. Do not give the address, the photographs or the landlord's details. If a claim has already been started through them, the firm supposedly handling it can be asked directly, and the FCA takes reports about businesses carrying on regulated activity without authorisation.

The losing move: handing over the details anyway because the call sounded official and the compensation figure sounded good.

What this does not cover

This is about checking who you are dealing with, not about whether a particular claim is worth bringing — that depends on the condition of the property, what was reported and when, and what the landlord did about it. It applies to England and Wales; claims management regulation works differently in Scotland and Northern Ireland. And if the disrepair is an immediate risk to health, that is not a claims question at all in the first instance: the council's environmental health team can act on a property regardless of who is handling any compensation claim, and Shelter publishes free guidance on getting repairs done.

One thing to do next: ask whoever contacted you for their registered company name and number, and search it on the register that applies to them before you send anything else.
